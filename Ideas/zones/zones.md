## Abstract Combat Zones in TTRPGs

In tabletop RPGs, **zones** are an abstract way of representing space during a scene without using exact measurements, grids, or miniatures. Instead of tracking whether someone is "27 feet away," the game divides the environment into meaningful fictional areas.

A zone is less a *precise location* and more a **tactically relevant space**.

Examples:

* "The ruined fountain"
* "The balcony"
* "Behind the parked trucks"
* "The ritual circle"
* "The north trench"
* "The burning hallway"

Characters move *between zones* rather than counting squares or meters.

---

# Core Concept

A zone system asks:

> "What areas of the scene matter tactically?"

rather than:

> "Exactly where is everyone standing?"

Zones simplify positioning while preserving:

* movement,
* range,
* cover,
* tactical choices,
* and spatial relationships.

---

# Typical Characteristics of Zones

## 1. Zones Represent Meaningful Areas

A zone usually corresponds to:

* a room,
* section of terrain,
* elevation level,
* cluster of cover,
* or tactical position.

Good zones are:

* easy to visualize,
* fictionally distinct,
* tactically interesting.

### Example

Instead of a warehouse map with measured distances:

| Zone         | Description                      |
| ------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Loading Dock | Crates, forklifts, open exterior |
| Catwalks     | Elevated metal walkways          |
| Office Block | Windows and hard cover           |
| Main Floor   | Open dangerous space             |

---

## 2. Movement Is Zone-to-Zone

Characters usually:

* move one zone freely,
* or spend an action to move multiple zones.

Movement becomes:

* crossing open ground,
* climbing levels,
* pushing into melee,
* retreating,
* flanking,
* repositioning.

not:

* counting movement speed in feet.

---

## 3. Range Is Relative

Weapons and abilities interact with zones abstractly.

Example:

| Weapon Type  | Effective Range  |
| ------------ | ---------------- |
| Knife        | Same zone only   |
| Pistol       | Adjacent zones   |
| Rifle        | Several zones    |
| Sniper rifle | Any visible zone |

This preserves tactical differentiation without geometric precision.

---

## 4. Multiple Characters Can Occupy One Zone

A zone is usually *not* a single point.

Several combatants may occupy:

* the same room,
* same trench,
* same rooftop,
* same patch of woods.

Within a zone, exact micro-positioning is abstracted.

---

## 5. Zones Emphasize Fictional Positioning

Zones work best when tied to narrative meaning.

Examples:

* "The zone is on fire."
* "This zone is flooded."
* "The balcony zone has elevation advantage."
* "Crossing the courtyard zone exposes you to machine gun fire."

The environment becomes easier to manage descriptively.

---

# Why Designers Use Zones

## Faster Play

No:

* counting squares,
* measuring templates,
* checking line-of-sight geometry.

This accelerates combat significantly.

---

## Theater-of-the-Mind Friendly

Zones provide:

* enough structure for tactics,
* without requiring maps.

Useful for:

* online play,
* narrative-heavy games,
* OSR,
* PbtA,
* cinematic combat.

---

## Easier GM Improvisation

A GM can sketch:

* 3–6 zones on paper,
* connect them with arrows,
* and begin immediately.

No battlemap prep required.

---

# Common Zone Structures

## Linear Zones

Useful for:

* chases,
* corridors,
* trench assaults,
* advancing fronts.

Example:

```text
[Street] — [Lobby] — [Stairwell] — [Roof]
```

This sounds close to the system you originally described.

---

## Networked Zones

Most common structure.

```text
          [Balcony]
              |
[Kitchen] — [Dining Room] — [Hall]
              |
          [Courtyard]
```

Movement follows connections.

---

## Layered / Vertical Zones

Useful for urban or military combat.

Example:

* Sewer
* Street
* Rooftops

Each becomes its own tactical layer.

---

# Tactical Depth Without Precise Geometry

A common misconception is that zones eliminate tactics.

Instead, they shift tactics away from:

* exact positioning,
* geometry optimization,
* movement counting,

toward:

* controlling chokepoints,
* managing exposure,
* securing advantageous terrain,
* narrative positioning,
* timing and pressure.

---

# Common Zone Rules

## Adjacency

Zones are often:

* adjacent,
* connected,
* or separated by obstacles.

Example:

* Adjacent = easy move
* Difficult connection = requires test/action

---

## Zone Traits

Zones may have properties:

| Trait        | Effect               |
| ------------ | -------------------- |
| Burning      | Damage over time     |
| Darkness     | Visibility penalties |
| Elevated     | Ranged advantage     |
| Dense Cover  | Harder to hit        |
| Contaminated | Requires protection  |

This works especially well in military or paranormal games.

---

## Engagement

Some systems distinguish:

* "same zone"
  from
* "engaged in melee."

This prevents absurdities where everyone in a room automatically fights hand-to-hand.

---

# Good Zone Design Principles

## Keep Zone Count Small

Usually:

* 3–7 zones total.

Too many recreates a grid system badly.

---

## Make Every Zone Matter

If two zones are tactically identical:

* combine them.

Zones should create meaningful choices.

---

## Name Zones Fictionally

Better:

* "Collapsed Chapel"

Worse:

* "Zone C"

Names reinforce immersion and memory.

---

# Examples in Actual Play

## Fantasy

```text
[Gate]
   |
[Courtyard] — [Wall]
   |
[Great Hall]
```

Archers on the wall.
Melee at the gate.
Wizard in the hall.

---

## Military / Paranormal

```text
[Forest Edge]
      |
[Kill Zone]
      |
[Research Facility]
   /          \
[Garage]   [Generator Room]
```

Now tactical choices emerge naturally:

* suppress the kill zone,
* flank through garage,
* destroy generator,
* breach facility.

---

# Philosophical Difference from Grids

Grid combat asks:

> "Where exactly are you?"

Zone combat asks:

> "What part of the situation are you influencing?"

That distinction is the heart of the approach.
